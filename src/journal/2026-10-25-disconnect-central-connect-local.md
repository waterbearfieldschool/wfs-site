The internet, as it's currently built, looks like it's about to become a much harder place to navigate safely. Our feeds are full of people saying "the internet is over."

Where our heads go is that the response is very close to the one we'd already reached for climate: **disconnect from centralized services as much as we reasonably can, while building connections at the local community level** — material connections *and* social ones. This project was worth doing anyway. Now it should accelerate. And there's a lot that's fun and joyful in it.

## Off-grid isn't extreme — it's normal

Being off the grid was the default for most humans for most of history, and it still is for a lot of people today. It only *feels* extreme because we're living at the far end of one particular, very plugged-in way of life.

Many people arriving at this question now come from lives that never expected to leave the grid, so the tone can sound pretty freaked out. Practically, though, it helps to put it in modest terms and **think of it like camping**. Then it's not so scary, and a small amount of effort up front buys a lot of resilience against short disruptions.

## Buy yourself a learning curve

A phrase we like: preparation **buys you a learning curve**. If the grid goes down for a week and you're ready for three, you're calm. You can spend that time talking with neighbors, gathering information, and planning for what happens if it stays down longer — instead of scrambling for basic needs.

## A simple map: needs × place × time

We think in terms of the basics — **food, water, shelter, heating and cooling, energy, and communications** — and imagine changing each one at different scales:

- **Place:** you and your family and friends → your block or apartment building → your town. (Maybe the bioregion, someday.)
- **Time:** what if you had to go without centralized services for **3 days? 3 weeks? 3 months? 3 years?**

| Unplugged for… | 3 days | 3 weeks | 3 months | 3 years |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Household** | camping kit | stores — the learning curve | garden, water source, heat | not alone |
| **Block / building** | know your neighbors | simple radio net, shared tools | shared power, water, comms | skills spread around |
| **Town** | — | mesh across town | food and trade links | real local capacity |

Move outward and forward one cell at a time.

## Why start with communications

Communications is one of the most important pieces: it gives you **situational awareness** — what's actually going on, quickly — and lets you **coordinate with others at a distance**. It's also one of the more technical pieces, which is exactly why a little hands-on help goes a long way.

- **Neighborhood scale:** simple radio is not hard to set up and works well.
- **Beyond the neighborhood:** that's where ham radio (licensed) or mesh radio comes in.
- **Going digital:** the real leap is sending *data*, not just voice. Ham emergency operators already send documents and email over radio, so you don't have to read lists of names aloud — the list just arrives. From there you can imagine local databases, shared files, and even local AI models.

## Subsidiarity for networks

There's an old organizing principle called **subsidiarity**: handle each issue at the most local level that can deal with it, and only bump it up a level when you have to. It turns out to be a good rule for building systems right now:

1. Keep what you can on your own devices and your local network.
2. Share across the neighborhood and town by radio and mesh.
3. Go out to the open internet **only when you need to, briefly, and carefully** — maybe through a separate "airlock" computer that's disconnected from the rest of your network while it's online, checked over afterward, with files carried back by USB stick. Something like an infection-control protocol, but for data.

People in the local-first and peer-to-peer world have been working on these ideas for years. Back then it felt like a niche, mostly theoretical concern. It doesn't anymore — and the tools have come a long way. (Reticulum, for example, already runs over cheap LoRa radio boards.)

## What we want to do: offline-first gatherings

We'd like to run gatherings — on the farm, and in town and city settings — built around a challenge:

> **No existing internet infrastructure allowed. Get some files from one side of town to the other.**

Teams bring Raspberry Pis running local Wi-Fi and set up mesh radio repeaters — on a rooftop between the two points, say — using a map, or by walking the route, to figure out where repeaters need to go to hold the connection. The same game works on a farm or in a suburb. We want to stay agnostic about the specific technology and try several side by side.

## Why this is a hopeful moment

In a way, this is one of the better timelines. The current wave of attacks is loud and easy to see. A worse version would be quiet and sneaky. Instead, lots of people have suddenly become aware of how fragile things are — and are curious about alternatives that are more local, more secure, and more under their own control.

If that's you, come find us. We'd love to set up radios, build some repeaters, and try sending files without the internet together.
